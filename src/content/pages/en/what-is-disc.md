## Background

We have four products that measure people's behaviour: what motivates them, what they dislike, which communication style they prefer and much more.

DISC theory is one of the world's most widely used tools for creating personal profiles. The theory and model were developed by Dr William Moulton Marston as early as the 1920s. PulsAnalys builds on Dr Marston's theories of the normal behaviour of healthy people (Marston, William M. 1928. *The Emotions of Normal People*).

Today the theory is often shown as a model with four factor bars, with the established names:

- **D** – Dominance
- **I** – Influence
- **S** – Steadiness
- **C** – Conscientiousness

## DISC theory

Dr Marston describes how people learn, and choose attitudes and behaviour, in relation to the environment they operate in. They can be mainly active or mainly reserved, depending on how they experience their surroundings.

We can take on every dimension at different times, but over time we tend to favour certain forms of behaviour – our basic behaviour – and give less weight to others. This pattern was originally thought to rest on both innate qualities and learned behaviour. So we are not only partly pre-programmed and able to learn, we are also sensitive to how we affect others and what they think of us.

Dr Marston argues that people develop and learn different patterns of action, that people have different qualities, and that this affects how they handle different work situations. Even though we can all show the four patterns at some point, it is reasonable to assume that we develop a style that favours some of them in particular.

Using these extremes, four typical interactions – or behavioural patterns – between a person and their environment emerge. The environment is described as either antagonistic (competitive and hostile) or favourable (friendly and accommodating).
